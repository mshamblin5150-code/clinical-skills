## Restatement

Prevention begins with a routine, patient-centered sexual history. New or multiple partners,
partners with a recent infection, inconsistent barrier use in networks with multiple partners,
transactional sex, and contact with people engaged in sex work can increase exposure. Adolescents,
pregnant patients, people with HIV, men who have sex with men, and transgender people need
screening and counseling that reflect their epidemiology and sexual practices without treating
identity itself as a diagnosis.

HIV testing should accompany evaluation for a possible sexually transmitted infection. Screening
for other infections is selected from the patient's anatomy, practices, exposures, and demographic
context. Prevention counseling can reduce infection rates and can occur in person, by telephone,
or through other media. A brief encounter can still be useful when it assesses the patient's
understanding of transmission, explores behavior and readiness for change, agrees on a realistic
goal, and identifies a concrete next step.

Vaccination prevents several infections transmitted sexually or associated with sexual activity.
Relevant immunization includes hepatitis A, hepatitis B, human papillomavirus, meningococcal, and
mpox vaccines when the patient meets the separate indications for each. Observational evidence has
suggested that one meningococcal vaccine might lower gonorrhea rates, but randomized trials have
not confirmed that effect; it should not be presented as established gonorrhea prevention.

Barrier strategies include external and internal condoms. External condom use is associated with
lower transmission of HIV, chlamydia, gonorrhea, herpes simplex virus, and human papillomavirus.
For selected men who have sex with men and transgender women with recent bacterial infection or
planned higher-exposure activity, doxycycline postexposure prophylaxis can reduce chlamydia,
gonorrhea, and syphilis. Selection and counseling belong to an individualized clinical discussion;
the strategy should not be generalized to populations for which benefit has not been established.

Antimicrobial prevention also includes HIV treatment as prevention, HIV preexposure and
postexposure prophylaxis, and suppressive antiviral therapy to reduce genital herpes transmission
in appropriate discordant couples. Partner services remain essential after diagnosis. Expedited
partner therapy is effective for heterosexual partners of patients with gonorrhea or chlamydia
when those partners cannot be directly evaluated, and it can reduce reinfection.

Male circumcision lowers HIV acquisition risk among heterosexual men and has also been associated
with reduced herpes simplex virus and human papillomavirus infection. No single intervention
replaces the others. Effective prevention joins a respectful exposure assessment, anatomy- and
practice-directed screening, vaccination, barrier use, selected prophylaxis, treatment of people
with infection, and partner management into one plan that the patient can realistically follow.
