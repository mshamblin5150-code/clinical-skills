## Restatement

Most *Chlamydia trachomatis* infections cause no symptoms. The cervix, urethra, and rectum are the
principal sites, and untreated infection may persist even though spontaneous clearance is
possible. Symptomatic urogenital infection can present as cervicitis with vaginal discharge,
abnormal bleeding, or purulent endocervical discharge. Infection may also involve the urethra or
rectum. Ascending cervical infection can produce pelvic inflammatory disease, whose abdominal or
pelvic pain and cervical, uterine, or adnexal tenderness may be subtle. Associated reproductive
sequelae include tubal infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain.

Urethritis commonly produces dysuria and a mucoid or watery discharge, while epididymitis presents
with unilateral testicular pain and palpable epididymal swelling and may coexist with asymptomatic
urethral infection. Extragenital disease includes rectal infection and proctitis. The serovars that
cause lymphogranuloma venereum can produce prominent anorectal pain, tenesmus, and discharge,
particularly among men who have sex with men, while other rectal infections are commonly silent.
Sexually acquired conjunctivitis generally causes nonpurulent conjunctival inflammation.
Chlamydia is also an important sexually transmitted trigger of reactive arthritis, and the
preceding genital infection may have gone unnoticed.

These syndromes cannot be reliably distinguished from similar illnesses caused by other pathogens
on clinical features alone. Microbiologic testing is therefore essential. Any sexually active
person with findings consistent with cervicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, urethritis,
epididymitis, or proctitis should receive diagnostic testing. Testing is also appropriate without
symptoms when population risk, sexual history, a concurrent gonorrhea diagnosis, or recent
chlamydia treatment increases the likelihood of infection. Screening policy for specific groups
is addressed separately from this diagnostic approach.

Nucleic acid amplification testing is the preferred method. Vaginal swabs are preferred for
female genitourinary testing, and first-catch urine is used for male genitourinary testing. Rectal
swabs are needed when rectal infection is being assessed, and patients can collect swab specimens
themselves when that option is available. Laboratory testing may not return during the encounter,
so patients with compatible signs or symptoms are often treated presumptively while confirmation
is pending. Point-of-care amplification testing can support organism-directed care during a single
encounter when available. Because gonorrhea produces overlapping syndromes and coinfection is
common, chlamydia testing generally should be paired with gonorrhea testing rather than treated as
an isolated diagnostic decision. Specimen choice must also follow the sites exposed; a genital
sample does not answer whether rectal infection is present. This site-directed approach matters
especially when infection is silent, because symptoms cannot reliably identify every infected
location or determine who remains capable of transmission.
