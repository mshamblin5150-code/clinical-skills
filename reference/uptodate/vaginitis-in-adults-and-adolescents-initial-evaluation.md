## Restatement

Normal vaginal discharge in a reproductive-aged patient may be white or clear, thick or thin, and
largely without odor. Estrogen supports glycogen in the vaginal epithelium, and lactobacilli
convert that glycogen into lactic acid. The resulting acidic environment helps preserve normal
flora and restrain potential pathogens. A change from the patient's normal discharge should be
interpreted with symptoms and examination findings rather than assumed to represent infection.

Infectious causes are more common than noninfectious causes. Bacterial vaginosis, Candida
vulvovaginitis, and trichomoniasis account for much of the infectious burden. Noninfectious
possibilities include menopausal atrophy, a retained foreign body, exposure to washes, douches, or
other irritants and allergens, dermatologic disease, and less common systemic disorders. Patients
may report altered discharge, itching, burning, irritation, redness, painful intercourse,
spotting, or dysuria. These symptoms overlap substantially among causes and do not justify
empiric treatment without evaluation.

The etiology should be established before therapy begins. Initial work includes a focused history,
physical examination, testing for vaginal and cervical infection, and reassessment after targeted
treatment. Office testing uses vaginal acidity and microscopy and can provide immediate, lower-cost
information when trained staff and equipment are available. A discharge specimen is obtained from
the vaginal wall and examined with saline and potassium hydroxide. When bedside findings do not
identify an organism but suspicion remains, culture or nucleic acid amplification testing can
extend the evaluation.

Laboratory antigen or amplification assays can also serve as the primary diagnostic approach for
bacterial vaginosis, candidiasis, or trichomoniasis. They are generally more sensitive and
specific than bedside testing but take longer and cost more. The choice between approaches should
reflect access, timing, local expertise, and the consequences of a missed diagnosis rather than a
presumption that either strategy is universally superior.

Sexually active patients also require consideration of gonorrhea, chlamydia, and trichomoniasis
because untreated sexually transmitted infection can progress to pelvic inflammatory disease.
HIV testing should be offered when the encounter is an STI evaluation or the patient has increased
acquisition risk. Suspected pelvic inflammatory disease, ulceration, fistula, or an obvious vulvar,
vaginal, or cervical malignancy changes the pathway and warrants prompt specialty care.

When the first evaluation does not identify a cause, a secondary assessment should occur,
preferably while symptoms are active. Prepubertal children, menopausal patients, people using
tamoxifen, and those with recurrent symptoms need population-specific evaluation or treatment.
The governing principle is diagnostic discipline: define the cause, recognize alarm findings,
and reassess unresolved symptoms instead of repeating unconfirmed therapy.
