## Restatement

An internal condom combines a sheath placed inside the vagina with an exterior frame and an inner
component that steadies the device. The version most readily available uses nitrile, although
polyurethane and natural-rubber designs have also existed. Once positioned, the sheath separates
genital secretions from the reproductive tract and provides the method's contraceptive barrier.

Protection depends on inserting the device properly whenever vaginal intercourse occurs. It lowers
the chance of pregnancy and may limit sexually transmitted infection, but infection data are much
less developed than for external condoms. Both ideal-use and everyday-use contraceptive outcomes
are less favorable than those of external devices. Counseling should state that difference while
recognizing that a method a patient can control and use consistently may fit a particular
relationship better.

Fertility resumes without a method-related delay, and obtaining the device requires neither an
examination nor individualized sizing. It gives the receptive partner an option when an external
device is refused or cannot be used. Nitrile is suitable for most people who react to latex, while
a nitrile reaction rules out that material. Some users find placement or removal difficult, so
demonstration, practice, and clear instructions matter.

Counseling should include how to recognize correct placement. The inner portion needs to remain
inside the vagina while the outer frame stays outside, and the penis must enter the sleeve rather
than pass beside it. A patient who is comfortable checking those points is more likely to detect a
placement problem before exposure. Difficulty during an early attempt does not mean the method is
unsuitable, but unresolved pain, repeated displacement, or inability to position the device should
prompt a different barrier choice.

Unlike an external device, it may be positioned before sexual activity and can remain briefly
after ejaculation. Its cost is usually higher, and it is not intended for anal use. Its broader
coverage should provide more infection protection than a diaphragm or cervical cap.

A fresh device belongs to each episode of vaginal intercourse, and advance placement may make use
easier. Either water- or oil-based lubricant is compatible, unlike oil applied to a latex external
condom. The internal and external methods must not be combined because rubbing between them may
shift or damage either barrier. Reuse is not advised because it lacks adequate study. The practical choice should therefore integrate
effectiveness, material tolerance, cost, partner cooperation, insertion comfort, and the user's
ability to employ the device correctly every time. The internal option is valuable chiefly because
it widens patient control over barrier protection. Its higher contraceptive failure rate relative
to external condoms and the thinner infection evidence should remain part of informed choice, not
reasons to withhold a method that may be used more consistently in the patient's circumstances.
