## Restatement

Pregnancy does not prevent ordinary viral or bacterial infection, and some infections become more
severe during pregnancy or threaten the fetus or newborn. Prevention combines hygiene, safer food
and travel choices, vaccination, reduction of sexual and environmental exposure, and prompt
clinical advice after a concerning exposure or illness.

Handwashing is central, especially after toileting, diaper changes, contact with a young child's
saliva or urine, handling raw food, animals, soil, garbage, or dirty laundry, and before eating.
Alcohol-based sanitizer is useful when soap and water are unavailable, but visibly dirty hands
need washing. Avoid sharing food, drinks, utensils, toothbrushes, or pacifiers with young children
because cytomegalovirus and other infections can spread through body fluids. Clean surfaces and
toys that contact saliva or urine.

Food precautions include avoiding unpasteurized products, raw batter, raw or undercooked meat,
fish, or shellfish, unpasteurized juice, raw sprouts, cold deli meats, and prepared meat or seafood
salads that may carry harmful organisms. Wash produce. Reduce toxoplasmosis exposure by avoiding
undercooked meat, using gloves with soil, and having someone else change cat litter when possible.
Rodents and their droppings should be avoided.

Mosquito protection includes appropriate repellent, protective clothing, screens, and netting.
Travel plans should be reviewed for malaria, Zika, yellow fever, and food- or waterborne risk, with
specialist travel advice when appropriate. If a partner has traveled to an area with Zika risk,
abstinence or condoms during pregnancy can reduce sexual transmission. Condoms also reduce other
sexually transmitted infections; avoid sex when a partner has discharge, dysuria, genital sores,
or other possible infection. Never share injection equipment.

Immunizations should be current before pregnancy when possible. Influenza, pertussis, COVID-19,
and hepatitis B vaccination are recommended during pregnancy, while live vaccines for rubella or
varicella are given before pregnancy or after delivery rather than during pregnancy. Household
vaccination reduces exposure for both parent and infant.

Contact a clinician after exposure to parvovirus, varicella, Zika, or another significant
infection, or when symptoms such as fever, rash, swollen nodes, marked fatigue, respiratory
symptoms, or a flu-like illness develop. Specific testing, monitoring, preventive immune therapy,
or treatment depends on the organism and timing. Prenatal screening also addresses HIV, hepatitis,
group B streptococcus, and other infections that can pass during pregnancy or birth. Infection and
treatment risks should be discussed together; necessary care should not be delayed solely because
the patient is pregnant. Prevention lowers exposure but cannot eliminate it, so counseling should
also give the patient a clear route for reporting illness and obtaining organism-specific advice
without delay.
