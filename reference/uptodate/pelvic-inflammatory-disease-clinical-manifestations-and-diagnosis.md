## Restatement

Pelvic inflammatory disease is an acute infection of the upper genital tract that can involve the
uterus, fallopian tubes, ovaries, or more than one of these structures. Its presentation ranges
widely. Symptomatic patients often have recent lower abdominal pain together with new vaginal
discharge or bleeding between periods. Fever and chills may occur, and perihepatitis can present
with pronounced right upper quadrant tenderness. Pelvic organ tenderness on examination is the
defining physical finding, but the overall illness may still be subtle.

Clinicians should consider pelvic inflammatory disease in any sexually active female patient with
pelvic discomfort and maintain a particularly high level of suspicion in adolescents and patients
with multiple sexual partners. Initial evaluation aims to make a presumptive clinical diagnosis,
identify findings that increase or decrease its likelihood, assess illness severity, and consider
other causes of pelvic pain. The history should include a sexual history, and the physical
assessment should include abdominal and pelvic examinations. Testing should include a pregnancy
test, microscopy of vaginal discharge when available, nucleic acid amplification testing for
chlamydia, gonorrhea, and *Mycoplasma genitalium*, HIV screening, and syphilis serology.

Imaging is not required for every uncomplicated presentation. It becomes important when the
patient is acutely ill, does not improve with empiric therapy, or has continuing diagnostic
uncertainty. In those situations, imaging helps identify complications such as a tubo-ovarian
abscess and evaluate alternative explanations for the pain. Other specialized studies can support
the diagnosis in selected encounters, including imaging with characteristic findings, laparoscopy
showing compatible abnormalities, or endometrial biopsy demonstrating endometritis.

Presumptive diagnosis is reasonable when a younger sexually active patient has lower abdominal or
pelvic discomfort together with tenderness elicited by moving the cervix or palpating the uterus
or adnexa, particularly when exposure risk is present. Waiting for certainty can allow preventable
reproductive injury, so this bedside threshold supports immediate empiric antimicrobial care.
Fever, cervical discharge or friability, numerous leukocytes seen during saline examination of
vaginal secretions, or a positive test for gonorrhea, chlamydia, or *M. genitalium* adds support but
is not a prerequisite. The strategy deliberately accepts some diagnostic uncertainty to reduce
the harm of delayed treatment. It pairs that low threshold with testing for accompanying
infections, assessment for pregnancy and competing causes, and a clear plan to reassess. Worsening
illness, failure to respond, or a plausible alternative diagnosis should prompt imaging or a more
specialized evaluation rather than simple repetition of the original assumption.
