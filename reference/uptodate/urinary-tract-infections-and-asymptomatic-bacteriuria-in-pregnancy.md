## Restatement

Bacteriuria is common during pregnancy and can progress from an asymptomatic state to symptomatic
urinary tract infection when it is not treated. Pregnancy-related smooth muscle relaxation and
ureteral dilation may make bacterial ascent from the bladder to the kidney easier, increasing the
risk of pyelonephritis. Untreated bacteriuria may also be associated with adverse pregnancy and
newborn outcomes. As in nonpregnant patients, *Escherichia coli* is the predominant organism in
both asymptomatic bacteriuria and symptomatic urinary infection.

All pregnant patients should be screened for asymptomatic bacteriuria early in prenatal care using
a midstream urine culture. Diagnosis requires significant bacterial growth in a patient who does
not have symptoms of urinary infection. Once identified, therapy should be selected from the
culture and susceptibility results. Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria lowers the risk that the
infection will ascend to the kidney and is associated with better pregnancy outcomes. The
antibiotics considered are the same general options used for cystitis, adjusted for pregnancy,
organism susceptibility, allergy history, and other patient factors.

New dysuria, urinary frequency, or urgency should raise concern for acute cystitis. Culture growth
in the setting of those symptoms supports the diagnosis. Treatment begins empirically and is then
narrowed or changed when culture and susceptibility results return. Nitrofurantoin or
trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole are presented as empiric options, while beta-lactams and fosfomycin
may also be used in suitable circumstances. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is generally avoided
early in pregnancy because of theoretical concern, although it remains a reasonable choice when
better alternatives are unavailable. Drug selection should therefore reflect both the infection
and the stage of pregnancy rather than applying one regimen across all encounters.

Flank pain, nausea or vomiting, fever, and costovertebral angle tenderness suggest acute
pyelonephritis, whether or not cystitis symptoms are also present. Bacteriuria in that clinical
setting confirms the diagnosis. Pyelonephritis can cause severe maternal illness, including sepsis
and respiratory failure, and can produce obstetric complications. Management includes hospital
admission and parenteral antibiotic therapy. Ceftriaxone is the preferred initial agent in the
topic, with broader alternatives considered when resistance is suspected. After clinical
improvement, treatment can transition to an oral agent chosen from the isolate's susceptibility
profile. Once the treatment course is complete, preventive antibiotic therapy for the remainder
of pregnancy is a reasonable strategy to reduce recurrence. Across asymptomatic bacteriuria,
cystitis, and pyelonephritis, culture-directed follow-through is central rather than optional.
Symptoms determine urgency, but they do not replace culture: screening finds silent disease,
culture confirms symptomatic infection, susceptibility results refine therapy, and clinical
response determines when inpatient treatment can safely change to oral care.
