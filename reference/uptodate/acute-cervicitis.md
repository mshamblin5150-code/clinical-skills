## Restatement

Cervicitis is inflammation of the uterine cervix, usually centered on the columnar epithelium of
the endocervical glands but sometimes involving the ectocervical squamous epithelium. Sexually
transmitted infection is the leading cause. Gonorrhea and chlamydia are the most common identified
infections, while *Mycoplasma genitalium* may explain a meaningful minority of cases. When testing
does not identify an infection, mechanical or chemical irritation, including exposure to a
diaphragm or vaginal douche, may be responsible. Some encounters remain without a clearly defined
cause after evaluation.

Typical findings are purulent or mucopurulent endocervical discharge and cervical friability,
shown by bleeding induced when the cervix is gently touched with a swab. Patients may also report
abnormal vaginal discharge, dysuria, urinary frequency, bleeding between periods, or bleeding
after intercourse. Evaluation combines a focused history, physical and pelvic examinations, and
testing for likely infectious causes. Testing should address chlamydia, gonorrhea,
*Trichomonas vaginalis*, and bacterial vaginosis, with additional testing guided by the clinical
picture and available assays.

The diagnosis is clinical when examination shows mucopurulent cervical discharge or friability;
laboratory results identify a specific infectious etiology rather than establishing cervicitis by
themselves. The topic favors empiric treatment when physical examination supports cervicitis,
instead of waiting to choose therapy solely from acquisition risk or organism-specific results.
Because chlamydia and gonorrhea are common causes, initial therapy addresses both unless reliable
information supports a narrower approach. Direct comparative evidence among reasonable treatment
strategies is limited, so the patient's circumstances and preferences remain relevant. Treatment
is offered even when examination findings occur without reported symptoms, with the goals of
relieving symptoms and preventing infection from ascending into the upper genital tract.

Partner management and follow-up depend on the organism found. Partners need treatment when the
patient has chlamydia, gonorrhea, or trichomoniasis so that transmission and reinfection are less
likely. Recurrent symptoms call for reassessment of re-exposure, adherence, treatment failure, and
other causes. Persistent cervicitis may warrant evaluation or treatment directed toward
*M. genitalium* when it has not already been excluded or treated. Chronic cervicitis describes
ongoing abnormal cervical findings after the usual infectious causes have been treated or ruled
out. It may continue with mucopurulent discharge, but it more often reflects a noninfectious source
and should not be managed as though an unconfirmed sexually transmitted infection necessarily
persists. Continued symptoms should reopen the clinical assessment rather than trigger automatic
repetition of the original regimen. The clinician should reconnect examination, microbiology,
exposure history, partner care, and the response to prior therapy before deciding whether the
remaining process is infectious, irritant, or chronic.
