## Restatement

External condoms are made from latex, natural membrane, or synthetic materials. Latex products
are inexpensive and protect against pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections, including
HIV, when used correctly. They are the preferred material for patients concerned about either
infection transmission or pregnancy. Oil-based lubricants can weaken latex and increase breakage,
while water-based and most silicone-based lubricants are compatible. Adding spermicide does not
give a meaningful advantage over an otherwise appropriate condom.

Natural membrane condoms are made from lamb intestine. Their pores may allow viruses to pass, so
they should not be selected when prevention of sexually transmitted infection is an objective.
Synthetic products provide an alternative for people with latex allergy or sensitivity and are
expected to provide pregnancy and infection protection similar to latex. Material choice should
therefore be tied to the user's allergy status, lubricant use, and prevention goals rather than
appearance or familiarity alone.

The device works as a barrier, limiting contact with semen, sperm, genital lesions, viral shedding,
and penile, vaginal, or anal secretions. Real-world effectiveness depends heavily on motivation,
experience, and correct use. Consistent use strongly reduces infections transmitted to or from the
penile urethra, including gonorrhea, chlamydia, trichomoniasis, hepatitis B, and HIV. It can also
lower the risk of infections spread through skin or mucosal contact, such as herpes, syphilis,
chancroid, and human papillomavirus, when the involved area is covered. Protection is incomplete
for exposed skin outside the condom.

Counseling should address when and how to use the device, how to discuss it with partners, and how
to make use a routine part of intercourse. A new condom is placed before any genital contact,
remains intact through intercourse, and stays in place until withdrawal. It is used for every act
of intercourse. These behaviors matter more than simply possessing condoms because delayed,
intermittent, or incorrect use leaves exposure windows.

Advantages include reversible pregnancy prevention, reduced transmission of many infections, and
other noncontraceptive benefits. The main practical disadvantage is the need for partner
cooperation, and latex allergy is the contraindication specific to latex products. If a condom
tears, slips off, or is applied incorrectly, the encounter should prompt timely consideration of
emergency contraception and infection postexposure measures. Counseling should cover these backup
options in advance so device failure leads to a plan rather than delay. Follow-up should reflect
the exposure, the partner's known infection status, pregnancy possibility, and the time since the
event. A barrier-method conversation is most useful when it includes both routine technique and a
specific response to foreseeable failure.
