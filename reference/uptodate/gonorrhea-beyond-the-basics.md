## Restatement

Gonorrhea is a sexually transmitted bacterial infection caused by *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*. It can
spread through oral, vaginal, or anal sexual contact and through shared penetrative sex toys;
ejaculation is not required. Toilet seats and ordinary objects do not transmit it. A new partner,
multiple partners, or another sexually transmitted infection increases risk.

Infection may involve the throat, urethra, rectum, cervix, or upper genital tract, and many people
have no symptoms. Possible findings include vaginal or penile discharge, vaginal itching or
bleeding, dysuria, rectal discharge or painful bowel movements, pain during intercourse, or pain
and swelling in one testicle. Throat infection is commonly silent. Because symptoms do not reveal
every infected site, clinicians need an accurate sexual history to select urine and oral, rectal,
vaginal, cervical, or urethral specimens. A positive or indeterminate home result requires prompt
clinical follow-up, and symptoms or a credible exposure still warrant evaluation after a negative
home result.

A gonorrhea diagnosis should prompt testing for other infections, including HIV, chlamydia,
trichomoniasis, and syphilis, with hepatitis testing when appropriate. Untreated cervical infection
can progress to pelvic inflammatory disease, tubal scarring, infertility, and ectopic pregnancy.
Epididymitis can threaten male fertility, and disseminated infection can affect the blood, skin,
and joints. During pregnancy, untreated infection can contribute to pregnancy complications and
can infect the newborn during delivery, especially the eyes.

Gonorrhea is curable with antibiotics. Intramuscular therapy is preferred for uncomplicated
infection, and concurrent chlamydia treatment is given unless testing has excluded it. Complicated
disease needs a longer or broader approach. Persistent symptoms after therapy require prompt
reassessment for resistance, treatment failure, reinfection, or another infection. Follow-up
depends on the infected site; throat infection requires confirmation of clearance, and later
retesting is important because prior infection does not confer protection.

Recent partners need evaluation and treatment, sometimes through partner-delivered therapy when
direct care is unlikely. Sexual activity should pause until the patient and partners have completed
the required post-treatment interval. Prevention includes condoms during every sexual encounter,
cleaning sex toys, avoiding sex when either partner has possible infection symptoms, and obtaining
screening based on exposure and risk. Doxycycline postexposure prophylaxis may be considered for
selected men who have sex with men and transgender women at increased risk, but effectiveness is
not established for every population and antimicrobial resistance remains a concern. That choice
belongs to individualized counseling rather than self-directed use. Prevention counseling should
also make clear that successful treatment does not create immunity; another exposure can produce
another infection and renew the need for testing and partner care.
