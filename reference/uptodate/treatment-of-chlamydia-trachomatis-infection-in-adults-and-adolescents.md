## Restatement

Treatment of *Chlamydia trachomatis* infection aims to relieve symptoms, prevent upper genital
tract and reproductive complications, reduce transmission, and prevent reinfection. Doxycycline
and azithromycin have strong activity against the organism, while selected fluoroquinolones also
work but are used less often. Penicillins may allow persistence in laboratory models, and
cephalosporins and sulfonamides have limited activity, so they should not be substituted merely
because they are familiar antibiotics.

Doxycycline is preferred for nonpregnant patients. Clinical symptom resolution may be similar with
azithromycin, but microbiologic cure is less reliable with azithromycin, particularly for rectal
infection and genital infection in male patients. Inadequately treated rectal infection in female
patients may also seed repeat genital infection. During pregnancy, azithromycin is preferred.
Short doxycycline exposure may be safe, but uncertainty remains and an effective alternative is
available, making azithromycin the more appropriate routine choice.

All diagnosed patients should be tested for gonorrhea and treated when that result is positive.
Screening should also address syphilis, HIV, and other infections indicated by the patient's
exposures and practices. Sexual activity should stop until any required post-dose interval has
elapsed or a multidose regimen is complete, symptoms have resolved, and partners have been treated.
This prevents a technically adequate course from being undermined by immediate re-exposure.

Partner management is a treatment component, not a separate courtesy. Recent partners and the
most recent partner outside the usual lookback interval should be tested and treated. When a
partner is unlikely to obtain direct care, expedited partner therapy can provide medication
through the index patient or a prescription sent for the partner. For men who have sex with men,
the convenience of expedited therapy should be weighed against losing an opportunity to test and
counsel for HIV and other coinfections.

Proof of cure is indicated during pregnancy, when symptoms persist, when adherence is uncertain,
and after azithromycin treatment of rectal infection. Everyone treated for chlamydia should undergo
later retesting to detect reinfection. A recurrent positive result is usually reinfection and is
managed with the same basic approach as an initial infection. Clinically important resistance to
the principal active drug classes has not been described, so a repeat infection should first
prompt assessment of partner treatment, adherence, and renewed exposure rather than an automatic
assumption of antimicrobial resistance. Effective care joins the correct agent with testing for
coinfection, a temporary pause in sexual activity, partner treatment, and planned follow-up. Each
part protects the others: medication clears the current organism, while counseling, partner care,
and retesting address the conditions that commonly produce another positive result.
