## Restatement

New pelvic discomfort over a relatively short period is a symptom rather than a diagnosis. It can
be centered in one location, spread across the lower abdomen or pelvis, or extend into the lower
back. The initial
differential should remain broad because gynecologic, gastrointestinal, and urologic disorders are
common sources, while musculoskeletal, vascular, and neurologic disease can present similarly.
Some causes are common and self-limited; others threaten life or organ function and require urgent
intervention.

The preliminary assessment is designed to identify emergencies before completing a detailed
diagnostic workup. Clinicians form an overall impression, review vital signs, obtain a focused
history, and perform a limited examination at the same time. Unstable vital signs, peritoneal
findings, or concern for conditions such as ectopic pregnancy or bowel perforation demand rapid
escalation. Pregnancy status should be established promptly in patients of reproductive potential
because pregnancy-related conditions change both the differential and the required speed of
triage.

After immediate threats and pregnancy-related emergencies have been addressed, evaluation turns
to common causes. The history should characterize where the pain is located, how it feels, how it
has evolved, and which symptoms accompany it, including fever or vaginal bleeding. Medical,
surgical, gynecologic, and other relevant background helps place those findings in context. The
general examination includes vital signs, overall appearance, and a careful abdominal assessment.
Pelvic assessment begins externally, then uses a speculum to view the vagina and cervix, a
bimanual technique to assess uterine and adnexal structures, and a rectal assessment. Laboratory
testing follows the history and examination rather than a single
uniform panel. Pelvic ultrasonography is appropriate for most patients.

When evaluation identifies a likely cause, treatment should target that condition and the patient
should be reassessed. Resolution of pain after the intervention may end the workup. Failure to
improve requires another search for emergency pathology because important findings, including
peritoneal signs, can emerge over time. Once dangerous conditions are again excluded, clinicians
should consider an atypical presentation of a common illness, worsening chronic disease, or a less
common pelvic or abdominal disorder.

Persistent pain without an identified or strongly suspected cause creates a less certain role for
surgery. The decision to explore, usually by laparoscopy, should be shared with the patient. That
conversation balances the procedural risks against the possibility of missing a diagnosis that
could be found and treated surgically. Reassessment is therefore an active part of the diagnostic
strategy, not merely follow-up after an unchanged first impression.
