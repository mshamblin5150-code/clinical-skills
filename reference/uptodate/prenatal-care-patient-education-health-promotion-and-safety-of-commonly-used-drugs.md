## Restatement

Education and health promotion are core parts of prenatal care rather than optional additions to
medical surveillance. Counseling should explain how and when to contact the health care team and
should address medications, nutrition, vitamin and mineral intake, appropriate weight gain,
immunization, exercise and other activity, healthy behaviors, common pregnancy concerns, and
intimate partner violence. These subjects should be revisited as pregnancy progresses because the
patient's questions, exposures, and practical needs may change.

Medication use during pregnancy is common, but evidence about maternal effects, fetal effects,
and pregnancy-specific dose adjustment is often limited. The same uncertainty can extend into the
postpartum period. Medication decisions therefore require an explicit comparison of the benefits
and harms of treatment with those of leaving the condition untreated. Clinicians should use the
best evidence available and communicate its strength and limitations, rather than presenting the
absence of reassuring data as proof that a drug is harmful or safe.

Avoid fetal drug exposure when it is clinically possible, with particular care early in pregnancy
when organ formation is occurring. Later exposure can still affect fetal structure, function, or
growth, so the concern does not end after early pregnancy. At the same time, stopping necessary
therapy can also harm the pregnant patient or fetus. The appropriate decision is individualized
and considers the illness being treated, the consequence of no treatment, the timing of exposure,
and the evidence available for reasonable alternatives.

When medication is needed, keep the regimen as simple as the condition allows. Use the fewest
medications necessary, choose agents with the strongest pregnancy safety experience, and limit
exposure to the least amount and shortest duration that remain effective. An effective older drug
with a substantial safety record is generally preferred over a newer agent when pregnancy data
for the newer option are sparse or absent. This principle does not make age alone a guarantee of
safety; it places greater weight on accumulated evidence when efficacy is otherwise acceptable.

Patients should be encouraged to contact their clinician with medication questions and before
starting a new drug or stopping one they already take. That instruction applies to prescribed and
nonprescribed products because self-directed changes can remove needed treatment or introduce an
avoidable exposure. Good prenatal counseling gives the patient a clear route for timely questions,
frames medication choices as risk-benefit decisions, and integrates those decisions with the
broader nutrition, activity, immunization, safety, and health-promotion work of prenatal care.
