## Restatement

Septic abortion is diagnosed clinically when uterine infection follows a pregnancy loss or
termination. Abdominal or pelvic pain, uterine tenderness, purulent vaginal discharge, bleeding,
and fever are key findings, although the combination varies. The true incidence is uncertain
because the diagnosis includes infection after spontaneous loss as well as medication or surgical
termination. Most infections arise from vaginal flora and may be polymicrobial, including
anaerobes and common gram-negative and gram-positive organisms.

The illness can deteriorate rapidly and become fatal, so evaluation and treatment must proceed
without delay. A patient with pelvic or abdominal pain, uterine tenderness, and fever after a
recent pregnancy or loss needs a targeted history, laboratory assessment, and ultrasonography.
The history should include menstrual and pregnancy information without assuming the patient knows
that a pregnancy occurred or is comfortable disclosing how it ended. Clinical uncertainty about
those details should not postpone stabilization or source control.

Treatment requires coordinated restoration of perfusion with intravenous fluid, broad intravenous
antimicrobial therapy, and urgent evacuation of infected material from the uterus.
Antibiotics are essential but cannot substitute for surgical source control. Unstable patients
need simultaneous airway, breathing, and circulatory support, vasoactive therapy when required,
prompt antimicrobials, and operative evacuation. This work is generally conducted in an intensive
care or operative setting because sequential management would create dangerous delay.

Stable patients also require rapid care. Fluid resuscitation should reflect bleeding, sepsis,
volume status, and response rather than a single fixed formula. Empiric antibiotics must cover a
broad range of likely organisms and can be tailored after cultures return; the topic favors
piperacillin-tazobactam for many patients while recognizing alternatives based on allergy,
availability, and cost. Symptomatic uterine infection requires urgent surgical evacuation even
when formal sepsis criteria are not met. Expectant or medication-only management of retained
infected tissue is generally inappropriate because the condition can become life-threatening.

After uterine aspiration, intravenous fluids and antibiotics continue with close, repeated
reassessment. Clinical improvement permits transition to routine postoperative care and later to
an oral antimicrobial regimen guided by cultures and response. Failure to improve, worsening
sepsis, respiratory distress, coagulopathy, organ failure, peritonitis, abscess, major vascular
injury, or uncontrolled hemorrhage calls for operative escalation. Laparotomy may be required to
address extensive infection, abscess, injury, or bleeding. Hysterectomy is reserved as a
life-saving intervention when infection or hemorrhage cannot otherwise be controlled. The central
principle is speed: resuscitation, antimicrobial therapy, and source control are complementary and
must not be allowed to delay one another.
