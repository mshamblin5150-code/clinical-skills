## A:

**Differential:**

*Ganglion cyst of the right thumb interphalangeal joint* — a small, discrete, **mobile**, non-tender swelling arising at a joint line, present 2 to 3 months without change, symptomatic only on direct impact. Mobility and the joint-line origin are the two findings that place it. **Favored.**

*Digital mucous (myxoid) cyst* — the characteristic interphalangeal-joint cyst of a woman over 60, arising from an osteoarthritic joint, and clinically overlapping the ganglion almost entirely. Argued as less likely only by the described **medial** rather than dorsal location, since a mucous cyst typically sits dorsally near the nail matrix; the exam records no nail-plate ridging, which would have supported it. **Close second, and would not change today's management.**

*Epidermal inclusion cyst* — mobility and slow, painless growth fit. Argued against by the **joint-line** location, which points to a synovial rather than an epidermal origin, and by the absence of a visible punctum. **Less likely.**

*Giant cell tumor of the tendon sheath* — the second commonest hand mass after the ganglion, and it presents at exactly this site and tempo. Argued against by the described **mobility**, since these lesions are typically firm and tethered to the tendon sheath. Not excludable on physical examination alone, and this is the entity that makes the specialist referral appropriate rather than optional. **Less likely, but the reason for referral.**

*Heberden/Bouchard-type osteophyte of the interphalangeal joint* — common in a 71-year-old woman and easily mistaken for a cyst. Argued against decisively by **mobility**: an osteophyte is bony hard and fixed to bone. **Less likely.**

*Cutaneous or soft-tissue malignancy of the hand* — **the patient's own stated concern, and it is answered rather than skipped.** Argued against by a 2 to 3 month course without growth, mobility, absence of fixation to skin or deeper tissue, intact and undiscolored overlying skin, no ulceration or non-healing surface, and no regional lymphadenopathy. Nothing on today's exam supports malignancy. **Least likely — but excision with histopathology at the referral visit settles the question definitively, which is the appropriate answer to a stated cancer fear.**

*Gouty tophus* — deposits at interphalangeal joints do occur and can be mobile. Argued against by no documented gout, no prior attack, no joint inflammation and no other tophaceous deposit. **Least likely.**
