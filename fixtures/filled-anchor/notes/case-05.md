## A:

Differential:

Cutaneous abscess of the left index finger with surrounding cellulitis, resolving on oral
antibiotics. Supported by the discrete area of swelling with active drainage, erythema confined to
the base of the wound, marked improvement after 2 days of dual oral antibiotic therapy, pain only
2/10, afebrile, and no proximal spread. **Favored.**

Paronychia. The process sits at the base of the wound on the digit with an established drainage
site, rather than tracking the lateral or proximal nail fold, and the nail plate is not described as
involved. Less likely.

Felon, pulp space abscess of the distal phalanx. The fingertip tingling raises it, but a felon is a
tense, throbbing, closed-space infection and this pulp is neither tense nor severely painful at
2/10, and there is an open draining site decompressing the process. Less likely.

Flexor tenosynovitis. Kept on the list only because he is diabetic and this is the diagnosis whose
miss costs a hand. Argued against by all four Kanavel signs being absent: no fusiform digit
swelling, no flexed resting posture, no tenderness along the flexor sheath, no pain on passive
extension. Less likely.

Herpetic whitlow. Argued against by purulent drainage from a discrete wound rather than grouped
vesicles on an erythematous base, and by clear improvement on antibacterial therapy, which would not
be expected. Less likely.

Osteomyelitis of the distal phalanx. Not excluded. Diabetes and the persistent fingertip
paresthesia keep it live, and no probe-to-bone test or radiograph has been performed. Argued against
for now by the marked clinical improvement and the short symptom duration. Less likely, but the
reason to re-image if improvement stalls.

Retained foreign body. He recalls no injury at all, which cuts both ways: an unwitnessed puncture is
exactly the mechanism that leaves a splinter behind. Cannot be excluded without imaging. Less
likely.

Diabetic sensory peripheral neuropathy contributing to the fingertip tingling. Plausible as a
contributor and consistent with his diabetes, but it does not explain the swelling, the drainage or
the erythema, and it does not account for a symptom localized to one fingertip adjacent to an
infected wound. Less likely as the sole explanation.

Acute viral upper respiratory infection, second problem. Supported by cough with nasal drainage,
afebrile, clear lung fields in the lobes present, no increased work of breathing and no change from
his respiratory baseline.

COPD exacerbation. Argued against by the absence of wheeze, absence of increased dyspnea, no
reported increase in rescue inhaler use, SpO2 at his expected post-lobectomy baseline and no
purulent sputum documented. Less likely.

Community-acquired pneumonia. Argued against by clear lung fields in every lobe present, afebrile,
normal respiratory rate for him and well appearance. Less likely, though a post-lobectomy COPD
patient with 90 pack-years has less reserve than most, so it is named rather than dismissed.
